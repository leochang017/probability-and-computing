## What I learned
Almost all of Chapter 1 was about counting. Using the naive approach, you determine a probability by dividing the number of desired (or successful) outcomes by the total number of possible outcomes. But the naive approach only applies if all of the outcomes are equally likely. So most of the effort is in counting correctly. The lectures covered the multiplication rule, permutations, and the binomial coefficient C(n, k), which gives the number of ways you can choose k items from n items without regard to order. The lectures also covered story proofs and the axioms of probability, which provide a general way of defining probabilities when the outcomes are not equally likely. Finally, I used inclusion-exclusion to calculate the probability that at least one event will occur among a group of possibly overlapping events. I applied this to finding the probability that a hand has no cards in some suit/

What really surprised me was the birthday problem. The birthday problem shows that only 23 individuals need to be present before it becomes more probable than not that there is at least one shared birthday. Once I realized that 23 individuals create 253 pairs, and any pair could be a match, the birthday problem seemed much less unexpected. I wrote a program to randomly generate groups of individuals and then compute how often at least two people in a group shared a birthday. I ran this program 100,000 times each for groups of 23, 50, and 70 individuals. For each group size, I obtained values very close to what I expected based on the exact formula: approximately 0.51 for 23 individuals, approximately 0.97 for 50 individuals, and approximately 0.999 for 70 individuals. Each value was within about 0.002 of its theoretical counterpart. The robbery problem, although it used smaller numbers, was essentially identical in concept. There were six robberies and six districts, so logically one would expect each district to receive one robbery. However, due to chance alone, over 98 percent of the time some district received more than one robbery.

## What was hard
I believe that, at first, understanding probability intuitively rather than simply memorizing formulas is going to be difficult. One example of this difficulty showed up in Chapter 1, specifically in problems where it wasn't clear whether order mattered. My most difficult question was splitting twelve people into teams. It was difficult for me because the teams had no names assigned to them. Therefore, the first solution that I came up with counted every possible grouping more than once. To fix this, I needed to divide by either two or three factorial (3!), to account for each possible permutation of the teams. The same issue arose with determining the number of possible poker hands containing two pairs. The two ranks must be selected together (and not separately), or else each possible poker hand is counted twice.

With regard to my program, the most confusing aspect was the line of code that determined whether there was a match between any two elements in a list of birthdays. Specifically, this line converted the list of birthdays into a set, compared the cardinality (number of unique elements) of this new set to the number of people in the original list, and determined whether there was a duplicate element (i.e., a match). While I had a basic understanding of sets prior to writing my program, I needed to develop a deeper appreciation for why comparing the sizes of these two data structures worked. Ultimately, I came to realize that sets cannot contain duplicate elements; therefore, if the sizes differ, there must be a duplicate element somewhere in the list.
